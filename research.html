Passing the Buck: The Effects of a Targeted Tax Cut for Pass-Through Businesses

What are the effects of tax cuts for business owners on employment? Exploiting the elimination of Kansas state taxes on pass-through income, this paper estimates the labor market effects of a large and sudden reduction in taxes on business owners. I find that the reform reduced total employment by 2.3%, with larger declines concentrated outside its intended beneficiaries. Pass-through businesses experienced modest employment losses but no decline in payroll, resulting in higher average earnings among retained workers. Nonprofit organizations, by contrast, experienced the largest and most persistent employment declines, consistent with a reduction in the relative tax advantage of non-profit status. Together, these results highlight that targeted tax cuts can have unintended consequences for non-beneficiaries.

The Compensating Wage Differentials of Remote Work

The theory of equalizing differences predicts a wage penalty for remote work if workers value it as an amenity, yet remote workers consistently earn more than otherwise comparable in-person workers. Using data from the Survey of Income and Program Participation, I document a remote work wage premium of about 13%. I revisit this puzzle and propose a contracting-based explanation. I develop a principal-agent model in which work modality serves as a screening device: because effort costs depend on whether the assignment matches a worker’s private type, modality alone perfectly separates types and the principal implements the first-best contract. When time allocations are exogenously constrained, the screening instrument disappears, and the principal must distort wages and output to maintain incentive compatibility. The constrained contract delivers a positive wage premium for remote workers without invoking productivity or selection.
